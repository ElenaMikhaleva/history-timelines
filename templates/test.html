Bayes' Theorem, Finally Making Sense

Notes · Aug 21 · 6 min read

The first time I saw Bayes' theorem written out, it looked like accounting — multiply this, divide by that. It wasn't until I stopped treating it as arithmetic and started treating it as updating a belief that it actually clicked.

Everything below is the explanation I wish someone had given me before the exam, not the one in the textbook.

The setup

Bayes' theorem has four moving parts, and almost all the confusion comes from mixing them up. The prior is what you believed before you saw anything new. The likelihood is how well the evidence fits a given hypothesis. The evidence is the raw, unconditional probability of seeing what you saw, across every hypothesis. And the posterior is what you believe after taking the evidence into account — the number everyone actually wants.

prior = your belief before new evidence walks in

The theorem itself is just a rule for keeping these four honest with each other. Change the evidence and the posterior has to move. Ignore the prior and you'll consistently overreact to weak evidence — which is exactly what happens in the classic test-accuracy trap below.

A prior isn't a guess you defend. It's a starting point you're required to update.

Worked examples

Two versions of the same idea — one clinical, one everyday.

1 The false-positive trap

A disease affects 1 in 10,000 people. A test for it is 99% accurate — it correctly flags 99% of sick people, and correctly clears 99% of healthy people. You test positive. What's the actual probability you're sick?

Out of 10,000 people: 1 is actually sick and almost certainly tests positive. Of the 9,999 healthy people, 1% — about 100 — test positive anyway. So roughly 100 positives are false for every 1 that's real.

≈ 1%, not 99%.

2 The spam filter

80% of your inbox is spam. The word "invoice" appears in 30% of spam and 2% of real mail. A new message contains "invoice" — how likely is it spam?

≈ 98% — the base rate of spam was already high, so the word barely had to work.

Both examples run on the same engine: start from the base rate, then let the evidence nudge it — not replace it.

always compute the base rate first

Where this goes wrong

Base rate neglect is the single most common mistake — treating the test's accuracy as the answer instead of an input to it. The second is updating all the way to certainty on one weak signal, when the honest move is a smaller shift and a request for more evidence.

Takeaway

Bayes' theorem isn't really about disease tests or spam filters. It's a discipline for how much a given piece of evidence is allowed to move you — no more, no less.

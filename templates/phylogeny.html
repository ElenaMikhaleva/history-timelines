Tree of Life

How Carnivora splits into its two living branches.

Carnivora order

~290 living species
  • Defined by shearing carnassial teeth, not diet — some carnivorans are omnivores or even largely vegetarian (the giant panda).
  • Arose among the mammal radiation that followed the mass extinction ending the Mesozoic era.
  • Splits into two suborders, distinguished largely by the shape of the ear bones (auditory bullae): Feliformia and Caniformia.

Feliformia

Catlike carnivorans

Felidae cats

41 species
  • Lions, tigers, cheetahs, and the domestic cat.
  • Nearly all species have retractile claws.
  • Found on every continent except Antarctica.

Hyaenidae hyenas

4 species
  • Spotted, striped, and brown hyena, plus the termite-eating aardwolf.
  • Despite the resemblance, more closely related to cats and mongooses than to dogs.

Herpestidae mongooses

~34 species
  • Small, agile hunters across Africa and southern Asia.
  • Some species, like the meerkat, are highly social.

Viverridae civets & genets

~30 species
  • Mostly nocturnal, tree-dwelling hunters of the Old World tropics.
  • Among the closest living relatives to the ancestral carnivoran body plan.

Caniformia

Doglike carnivorans

Canidae dogs, wolves & foxes

~37 species
  • Wolves, foxes, jackals, and the domestic dog.
  • Found on every continent except Antarctica.

Ursidae bears

8 species
  • The largest living carnivorans.
  • Includes the giant panda, whose bamboo diet is a striking exception to the order's meat-shearing teeth.

Mustelidae weasels, otters & badgers

~60 species
  • The most species-rich carnivoran family.
  • Includes weasels, otters, badgers, and wolverines.

Pinnipedia seals, sea lions & walrus

~33 species
  • Fully aquatic caniforms descended from bear-like ancestors.
  • Includes true seals, eared seals, and the walrus.

Procyonidae raccoons & relatives

~14 species
  • Raccoons, coatis, and kinkajous.
  • Mostly found in the Americas.
